Chapter One — Signals in the Noise

It started with quantum noise.

The first hard problem I loved didn't want to be solved. At 18, I was simulating quantum sensors at JHU's Applied Physics Lab, hunting a signal buried so deep in noise the math said we'd never see it. We surfaced it anyway — and two publications later I'd learned the lesson that still runs everything I build: the interesting problems are the ones hiding inside the mess.

Chapter Two — Systems at Scale

Then I learned how big systems really work.

Research taught me to find signals; industry showed me what happens when a million of them arrive at once. Summers at Northrop Grumman — building tools that made hardware teams faster — taught me to love the machinery behind the product. Then AWS handed me the real thing: first linking Face ID and Touch ID to the apps people use every day, now replicating live identity across Regions so that when one goes down, nobody even notices. The best infrastructure is the kind no one has to think about.

Chapter Three — The Bigger Picture

And that building alone isn't enough.

Somewhere between shipping features, I noticed the technology was never the whole problem. BAE's riskiest changes slipped through because a process was broken, not a program. AlgenAir's next customers were hiding in its own sales data. So I learned the other half of the craft — consulting, leading 500 students as Gemstone's president, advising 18 capstone teams for Meta, Fannie Mae, and Lockheed Martin — and it always came down to the same move: listen first, then build what people actually need.
